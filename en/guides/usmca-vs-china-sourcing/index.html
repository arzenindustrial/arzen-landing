USMCA vs. China sourcing: real cost and lead-time numbers

What tariffs, freight and lead time actually look like when you compare a USMCA-qualifying Mexican supplier to a shop in China, for tooling and structural aerospace parts.

Key takeaways
  • Tariffs: China-origin parts carry tariff exposure (Arzen estimate on 2026-09-18: roughly 33% effective; rates change often); parts that qualify for USMCA preference can enter at 0% duty.
  • Freight: typically 14–21 days port to port by sea from China vs. overland by truck from Querétaro or Nuevo León — from a few hours to about a day of driving to the border, plus customs time.
  • Total landed cost often favors Mexico once tariffs, freight and inventory carrying cost are included, even at a higher unit price.
  • Verification risk is separate from geography — an unverified supplier in either country is a risk.
See verified Mexico-based suppliers

Procurement teams comparing a Mexican tooling shop to one in China usually start with unit price. That's the wrong first number. The invoice price is only one line item in what actually lands on your dock, and tariffs plus freight timing change the comparison more than most quotes suggest.

Tariff exposure

Parts sourced from China carried an effective tariff exposure of roughly 33% in Arzen's 2026-09-18 estimate (rates change frequently), on top of freight and customs handling. Sourcing from a USMCA-qualifying supplier in Querétaro or Nuevo León avoids that exposure entirely — the 0% figure is not a promotional number, it's the direct result of USMCA rules of origin for qualifying manufactured goods.

Freight and lead time

Sea freight from China typically runs 14 to 21 days port to port, before accounting for port congestion or customs delays. Overland trucking from Querétaro or Nuevo León to the U.S. border takes from a few hours to about a day of driving, plus customs processing. For a procurement team managing safety stock or a tight production schedule, that difference changes how much working capital sits in transit at any given time.

Certification cycle

Qualifying a new flight-critical prime supplier can take 12 to 24 months regardless of geography. Arzen's network stays deliberately narrow — tooling, fixtures and secondary structural components — precisely so buyers can move faster without that certification cycle, while still requiring in-person verification before any introduction.
