How to vet a CNC supplier in Mexico: a buyer's checklist

What a procurement team should actually check before committing production volume to a shop in Querétaro or Nuevo León — beyond a self-reported capabilities page.

Key takeaways
  • Don't trust a profile alone: equipment lists and certifications on a website are self-reported and unverified.
  • Visit in person or use a partner who has — a shop unwilling to host a visit is the clearest red flag.
  • Require a physical sample part at your real tolerances before any production commitment.
  • Check references with the shop's existing clients, not just the ones they hand-pick.
Skip the vetting — see pre-verified shops

A Google search returns dozens of CNC shops in Querétaro and Nuevo León claiming aerospace-grade capability. What it can't tell you is which of them can actually hold your tolerance, hit your delivery date, and answer the phone when a batch comes back out of spec. That gap is exactly where vetting has to happen before, not after, the first purchase order.

1. Confirm the equipment list against real parts

A shop's equipment list on paper means little without proof it's being used at the tolerances you need. Ask to see the specific machines running a part comparable to yours — same material family, similar tolerance band — not a generic tour. A 5-axis mill sitting idle in a showroom corner is not the same as a 5-axis mill in daily production.

2. Require a physical sample, not a quote

A fast quote is easy to produce. A physical sample part that passes your inspection is not. Before committing to a production run, get a sample made to your actual drawing and tolerances, and inspect it the same way you would a delivered batch.

3. Cross-check references — don't rely only on the ones you're given

Every shop will offer you its best reference. Ask for two or three additional current clients, and ask specifically about on-time delivery and how the shop handled a nonconformance, not just "would you recommend them."

4. Visit the shop floor in person

Photos and video calls can be staged. An in-person visit — walking the floor, checking work-in-progress against real job travelers, seeing how inspection is actually performed — is the single highest-signal step in vetting a supplier, and the one most buyers skip because of the time and cost of international travel.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to vet a CNC supplier in Mexico?

A thorough in-person verification — shop visit, equipment check, reference calls — typically takes 1 to 3 weeks once a shop is identified, depending on scheduling and travel to Querétaro or Nuevo León.

What's the biggest red flag when vetting a Mexican CNC shop?

A shop that won't let you visit in person, or that can't produce a sample part matching your tolerances before you commit to a production order, is the clearest warning sign.

Do I need to visit the shop myself, or can a third party verify it?

Either works, as long as the visit is genuinely in-person and documented. Many procurement teams use a verification partner like Arzen specifically to avoid the cost and time of international travel for every candidate shop.
